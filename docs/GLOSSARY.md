# Plain-language glossary

What each measure means, what to call it, and why a reader should care.

## Out of line for this company  — *robust z-score against the filer's own trailing baseline (signals_v3.robust_z)*

The whole idea: we compare a company's latest number only against that same company's own recent numbers, so we are asking "is this unusual for them?" and never "is this number big?".

**Why it matters:** A supermarket that always collects cash in 4 days and a builder who always waits 90 days are both normal for themselves; what is worth noticing is the day either one starts behaving unlike its own past. Every alert in this tool is that comparison and nothing else. It is also why an alert is never a judgement about the business — only a statement that the pattern changed.

**Example:** A company's gross margin has sat between 40% and 42% for the last 16 quarters, with a typical wobble of about 0.9 percentage points. This quarter it prints 38.2%. That is roughly 3 wobbles below its own normal, so the tool says gross margin is out of line for this company — even though 38.2% would be a perfectly healthy margin at a different company.

## Flagged  — *Verdict.gated_in / score >= THRESHOLD (45.0) and len(flags) >= MIN_FLAGS (2)*

The tool marks a company only when at least two different measures are out of line at once and their combined weight passes the cut-off.

**Why it matters:** One odd number is usually noise or an accounting quirk. Requiring two unrelated things to move together is what stops a single strange print from raising an alarm on its own. Note that on the real test this rule still missed most of the companies it was aiming at, so being flagged is a prompt to look, not a finding.

**Example:** Days-to-get-paid is 2.4 wobbles high and unsold stock is 2.1 wobbles high; together they score 51 out of 100, above the cut-off of 45, and there are two of them, so the company is flagged. If only the stock measure had moved, no flag — one is never enough.

## Concern score  — *Verdict.score (0-100, THRESHOLD 45.0, SCORE_DIVISOR 2.2178)*

A 0-100 number that adds up how far out of line each measure is, each one weighted by how much it helped on the practice data.

**Why it matters:** It is only a way of ranking which companies to read first. It is not a probability, not a grade, and not calibrated to anything a reader can act on: on the sealed test the score caught 29% of the deterioration events it was aiming at, against a target of 60%.

**Example:** Two measures fire at 2.4 and 2.1 wobbles; their weights are 0.38 and 0.29; the arithmetic gives 51.0 out of 100. The tool prints every input so the 51.0 can be re-added by hand.

## Profit not backed by cash  — *accrual_ratio = (TTM net income - TTM operating cash flow) / total assets*

How much of the last year's reported profit never arrived as actual cash, measured against the size of the company.

**Why it matters:** Profit is an opinion about timing; cash is a fact. When the gap between the two widens for a company that normally keeps them close, it usually means revenue is being recorded earlier, or costs later, than the money moves. It is the single oldest warning sign in accounting.

**Example:** Last four quarters: reported profit $400m, cash actually generated by the business $250m, total assets $3,000m. The $150m difference over $3,000m is 0.05. If this company's own usual figure is 0.01, that is a large move for them.

## Sales growing faster than cash  — *cash_conversion_gap = revenue YoY growth - operating cash flow YoY growth*

The difference between how fast sales grew this quarter versus a year ago and how fast the cash from running the business grew over the same stretch.

**Why it matters:** Healthy growth drags cash along with it. When sales accelerate and the cash does not follow, the growth is sitting in unpaid bills, unsold stock, or accounting timing rather than in the bank.

**Example:** Sales up 18% on the same quarter last year; cash from operations up 2%. The gap is 16 percentage points. For a company whose gap normally sits near zero, that is the flag.

## Unpaid customer bills growing faster than sales  — *receivables_vs_revenue = receivables YoY growth - revenue YoY growth*

Whether the money customers still owe is growing faster than the sales that created it.

**Why it matters:** If billings rise 25% while sales rise 10%, the extra sales are not being collected. That happens when a company loosens payment terms to close deals, ships to distributors who have not sold anything on, or has customers in trouble.

**Example:** Customers owed $500m a year ago and owe $625m now (+25%); sales grew 10%. The measure is +15 percentage points.

## Unsold stock growing faster than sales  — *inventory_vs_revenue = inventory YoY growth - revenue YoY growth*

Whether goods sitting in the warehouse are piling up faster than the company is selling.

**Why it matters:** Stock building ahead of sales is either a bet on demand that has not arrived or product nobody wants. Either way it usually ends in discounting, which shows up later as a margin drop.

**Example:** Warehouse stock up 30% year on year while sales are up 6%: +24 percentage points. If this company normally runs stock and sales within a few points of each other, it is out of line for them.

## Days to get paid  — *dso = receivables / TTM revenue * 365 (days sales outstanding)*

How many days, on average, between making a sale and collecting the money.

**Why it matters:** The number itself is meaningless across companies — some industries pay in a week, some in a quarter. What matters is a company's own figure drifting upward, which means customers are slower, terms were relaxed, or some of what was booked may not be collectable at all.

**Example:** Customers owe $500m; sales over the last four quarters were $4,000m. 500 / 4,000 x 365 = 46 days to get paid. If this company has run at 38-40 days for three years, 46 is a real change.

## Days of stock on the shelf  — *dio = inventory / TTM cost of revenue * 365 (days inventory outstanding)*

How many days of selling it would take to clear the goods currently in the warehouse.

**Why it matters:** Rising days of stock means goods are moving more slowly than the company is buying or making them. Stock that sits gets written down, and cash is tied up in it in the meantime.

**Example:** Stock on hand $300m; cost of the goods sold over the last four quarters $2,000m. 300 / 2,000 x 365 = 55 days of stock. Up from a steady 45 days, that is roughly a fifth more product sitting still.

## Work paid for but not yet delivered, versus sales  — *deferred_vs_revenue_gap = deferred revenue YoY growth - revenue YoY growth*

Whether money customers have paid in advance — subscriptions, contracts, prepaid services — is growing more slowly than the sales the company is currently reporting.

**Why it matters:** Money taken in advance is tomorrow's revenue arriving early, so it is one of the few forward-looking figures in a filing. When it thins out while reported sales still look fine, the current quarter may be living off a backlog that is not being refilled. Honest caveat: on the test data this measure pointed the wrong way, so its weight is set to zero and it currently contributes nothing to the score.

**Example:** Prepaid customer money up 3% year on year while reported sales are up 20%: a 17-point shortfall. The sales are being recognised faster than new commitments are coming in.

## Change in the growth rate  — *revenue_accel = revenue YoY growth this quarter - revenue YoY growth last quarter*

Not how fast sales are growing, but whether that growth rate itself sped up or slowed down since last quarter.

**Why it matters:** Growth slowing is normal; growth slowing abruptly, for a company whose growth rate is usually steady, is the shape that shows up before demand problems become obvious. A company can still be growing 12% and be decelerating hard.

**Example:** Sales grew 20% year on year last quarter and 12% this quarter. The change is -8 percentage points. For a company whose growth rate normally moves by 1-2 points a quarter, that is a break in pattern.

## Profit left per dollar of sales, before overheads  — *gross_margin = gross profit / revenue, same quarter*

Of every dollar of sales, how much is left after paying only for making or buying the thing that was sold.

**Why it matters:** This is the cleanest read on pricing power and input costs. It falls when a company discounts to keep volume, when materials or freight get more expensive, or when the sales mix shifts toward cheaper products. A sharp drop is also one of the five things this tool counts as confirmed deterioration.

**Example:** Sales $1,000m, cost of the goods sold $620m, so $380m is left: a 38% gross margin. A year ago it was 43%. The 5-point fall is enough to count as a margin collapse.

## Profit left per dollar of sales, after running costs  — *op_margin = operating income / revenue, same quarter*

Of every dollar of sales, how much survives after both the cost of the product and the cost of running the company — salaries, marketing, R&D, rent.

**Why it matters:** It shows whether overheads are being controlled as the business changes size. A company can hold its product margin steady and still slide here by spending harder to keep sales moving.

**Example:** Sales $1,000m, operating profit $80m: an 8% operating margin, down from 14% a year earlier.

## Cash kept per dollar of sales  — *ocf_to_revenue = TTM operating cash flow / TTM revenue*

Of every dollar of sales over the last year, how much actually ended up as cash from running the business.

**Why it matters:** It is the practical test of whether reported sales turn into money. When it slips for a company that has been stable, sales are being funded by the balance sheet — unpaid bills and stock — rather than converted.

**Example:** Sales over four quarters $4,000m; cash generated by operations $320m. That is 8 cents of cash per dollar of sales, down from a steady 15 cents.

## Years of cash flow needed to clear the debt  — *net_debt_to_ttm_ocf = (total debt - cash) / TTM operating cash flow*

How many years of the current rate of cash generation it would take to pay off borrowings after using up the cash already in the bank.

**Why it matters:** It says how much room a company has if trading gets worse: two years of cash flow is manageable, eight is not. Only computed when the business is generating cash at all. Honest caveat: like the prepaid-work measure, this one pointed the wrong way on the test data and its weight is set to zero, so it contributes nothing to the score today.

**Example:** Borrowings $2,400m, cash in the bank $400m, so net debt is $2,000m. Cash from operations over the last year was $500m. 2,000 / 500 = 4.0 years.

## Growth in the number of shares  — *dilution_yoy = YoY change in diluted share count (CORPORATE_ACTION_THRESHOLD 0.50)*

How much the company's share count grew over the year, which is how much each existing owner's slice shrank.

**Why it matters:** Shares issued to raise money or to pay staff quietly reduce every existing holder's claim on the same profits. A company issuing shares faster than it used to is often funding itself in a way its cash flow no longer can. Moves larger than 50% are ignored on purpose: those are stock splits or IPO conversions, not dilution.

**Example:** Share count goes from 102m to 111m: 8.8% more shares, so a holding that was 1.00% of the company is now 0.92% of it without anyone selling anything.

## A confirmed bad turn  — *deterioration event: label.py, MIN_CRITERIA=2 of 5 within HORIZON_QUARTERS=4*

The thing the tool is trying to see coming: at least two of five specific, checkable bad events showing up in a company's own later filings within a year.

**Why it matters:** This is the answer key, and it is deliberately not the share price. It is built only from what the company itself later reported, so it cannot be argued with and it does not depend on what the market was doing that year. Requiring two events at once means the company is breaking in more than one place, not just having one weak quarter.

**Example:** In the four quarters after a given date, a company's growth falls 19 points below its own recent norm AND its cash from operations halves. Two criteria, so that quarter is recorded as the moment it went wrong.

## Growth fell off a cliff  — *REVENUE_DECEL (REVENUE_DECEL_PP = 0.15)*

Sales growth came in at least 15 percentage points below what this same company had been averaging over its previous four quarters.

**Why it matters:** Measured against the company's own recent growth rate, not a fixed number, so a business that has always grown slowly is not permanently branded as deteriorating.

**Example:** A company averaging 22% growth over the last four quarters prints 4%. An 18-point drop clears the 15-point bar.

## Margin dropped sharply  — *MARGIN_COLLAPSE (GROSS_MARGIN_DROP_PP = 0.05)*

The share of each sales dollar left after product costs fell by at least 5 percentage points versus the same quarter a year earlier.

**Why it matters:** Compared against the same quarter last year, so seasonal businesses are not caught out by their own calendar. A drop this size usually means sustained discounting or a cost shock that could not be passed on.

**Example:** Gross margin 36% this quarter against 43% in the same quarter last year: a 7-point fall.

## Cash from operations broke down  — *OCF_NEGATIVE / OCF_HALVED (OCF_DECLINE_FRAC = 0.50)*

Cash generated by the business over the last four quarters either turned negative, or fell by half or more against the same four quarters a year earlier.

**Why it matters:** Turning negative means the day-to-day business is now consuming money instead of producing it, which has to be funded by borrowing or by selling shares. Halving is the milder version of the same problem.

**Example:** Cash from operations was $480m for the year to last September and $190m for the year to this September: down 60%, past the halving bar. Had it come in at minus $30m, it would count as turned negative instead.

## Big writedown  — *IMPAIRMENT (IMPAIRMENT_OF_ASSETS = 0.05)*

The company admitted in its own accounts that something it owns is worth less than the books said, by an amount worth at least 5% of everything it owns.

**Why it matters:** A writedown is management conceding that an acquisition, a factory, or a product line will not earn what was expected. It is backward-looking by nature — the value was already gone — but it is unambiguous and self-declared.

**Example:** A $410m writedown against $6,200m of total assets is 6.6%, past the 5% bar.

## The numbers were changed after the fact  — *RESTATEMENT (any vintage with form ending in "/A")*

The company refiled an amended version of a past sales, cash-flow, or profit figure.

**Why it matters:** It is the strongest confirmation available that the originally published numbers did not hold, stated by the company itself rather than inferred by anyone.

**Example:** A 10-K/A filed in March 2023 revises the sales figure originally reported for the quarter ending June 2022.

## Typical wobble  — *median absolute deviation (MAD), scaled by 1.4826*

How much a company's number usually bounces around from quarter to quarter, measured in a way that one freak quarter cannot distort.

**Why it matters:** The everyday way of measuring spread gets wrecked by a single extreme quarter — and after one blow-up it would take years for a company to look abnormal again. This takes the middle of the bounces instead, so one wild quarter is just one wild quarter.

**Example:** Sixteen quarters of gross margin, mostly 40-42%, plus one pandemic quarter at 24%. The ordinary measure of spread would report a wobble of about 4 points and go half-blind. This one reports about 0.9 points — the wobble that actually describes normal quarters.

## The company's own recent normal  — *baseline (MIN_HISTORY=12, MIN_BASELINE_N=8, MAX_BASELINE=20)*

The stack of that company's own past quarters that the current one is judged against — at most the last 20, and never fewer than 12 quarters of history overall with 8 usable readings for the particular measure.

**Why it matters:** Too short a history and ordinary variation looks like a break; too long and the baseline describes a business that no longer exists. Companies that cannot meet the minimum are set aside rather than scored on thin evidence.

**Example:** A company that listed two years ago has 8 quarters of history. Below the 12-quarter minimum, so the tool declines to assess it and says why, rather than guessing.

## Minimum wobble allowed  — *scale floor (per-metric third element of TRACKED)*

A floor under how small a company's typical wobble is allowed to be, set separately for each measure at roughly the size of ordinary measurement noise.

**Why it matters:** Without it, a company with an unusually flat stretch would have a wobble near zero, and then any ordinary move would read as a once-in-a-lifetime event. The floor stops a quiet period from manufacturing alarms. When it is applied, the output says so — the flag is marked scale floored.

**Example:** Days-to-get-paid has a floor of 2 days. A company whose collection period has been pinned at 41 days for three years gets a wobble of 2 days rather than 0.3, so a move to 44 days reads as 1.5 wobbles, not 10.

## Only what was public that day  — *point-in-time (edgar.as_of, truncation on `filed`)*

When the tool looks at a past date, it uses only the figures that had actually been published by then — not the corrected versions that came out later.

**Why it matters:** It is the difference between a real test and a rigged one. Restated figures make old quarters look obviously broken in hindsight; a reader at the time did not have them. Because the historical test and the live tool use exactly the same code, a result from one means something for the other.

**Example:** A quarter ending 31 March is not published until 10 May. Ask the tool what it thought on 30 April and that quarter simply does not exist yet, even though the file on disk contains it.

## Version of a number  — *vintage (list of filed versions per fact)*

Each figure is kept as the full sequence of versions the company published for it — the original, then every later correction — each stamped with the date it appeared.

**Why it matters:** It is what makes "only what was public that day" possible, and it is how the tool spots restatements at all. A figure that was revised three times is three different pieces of information, not one.

**Example:** Sales for the quarter ending June 2021: first published 5 August 2021 at $412m, revised 21 February 2022 to $398m. A reader in December 2021 saw $412m, so that is what the tool uses for December 2021.

## Last four quarters combined  — *trailing twelve months (TTM, derive.ttm, contiguity-gated)*

A full year's worth of a figure, made by adding the four most recent quarters, which smooths out the fact that most businesses have busy and quiet seasons.

**Why it matters:** Comparing one quarter to the next is mostly a comparison of seasons. Adding four consecutive quarters removes that. Crucially, the tool refuses to add four quarters that are not actually consecutive: if a quarter is missing it returns nothing rather than a plausible wrong total.

**Example:** Q3 2024 through Q2 2025 add to $4,000m of sales. If Q4 2024 is missing from the record, the tool does not quietly reach back to Q3 2023 to make four — it declines to produce the figure.

## How complete the record is  — *coverage (derive.coverage, COVERAGE_MIN = 0.90)*

The share of a company's reported quarters for which a particular figure is actually present; below 90% the tool will not use that figure.

**Why it matters:** A gappy record produces confident nonsense: a stock figure from 2020 divided by costs from 2018 once produced a perfectly plausible-looking 218 days of inventory. Refusing to score is the honest answer, and the tool says which figure was missing and how much of it.

**Example:** Sales appear in 40 quarters, cash from operations in only 34. That is 85%, below the 90% bar, so this company is reported as not assessable with the reason given, rather than scored on the two-thirds of measures that need cash flow.

## Cannot be assessed, and why  — *scoreable=False and Verdict.reason*

When a company cannot be judged, the tool says so plainly and names the obstacle instead of returning a low score.

**Why it matters:** A quiet zero is indistinguishable from a clean bill of health. "We cannot assess this company because its cash-flow figures have gaps" tells a reader something true; a score of 0 would tell them something false.

**Example:** Typical reasons in the output: not enough of its own history yet (9 quarters of the 12 needed); cash-flow figures present for only 85% of quarters; no filings yet published as of this date.

## Worked out rather than reported  — *derived quarter / derived_fraction*

Most companies publish cash-flow figures as a running year-to-date total, so single quarters have to be recovered by subtraction, and the tool reports what fraction of a reading rests on that.

**Why it matters:** A subtracted figure is arithmetic on two published numbers, not a third published number. It is sound, but a reader deserves to know how much of a conclusion depends on it. Each such quarter is also treated as public only once both of the figures it was derived from were public.

**Example:** A company reports six months of cash flow as $180m and three months as $70m. The second quarter alone is $110m — correct, but derived. If 30% of a reading's inputs are derived, the output says 0.30.

## Whether a company qualifies for the test  — *admission (universe.admit)*

The set of yes/no rules deciding whether a company can be part of the evaluation at all, with every rejection recorded and its reason printed.

**Why it matters:** Companies quietly dropped from a test are how flattering results get made. Recording each rejection means the population being measured is visible. Banks, insurers and property companies are excluded outright, because every measure here — stock, unpaid bills, product margin — either means something different for them or is not reported.

**Example:** Rejections read like: excluded sector (SIC 6022); fewer than 12 quarters of history since electronic filing began; cash-flow figures cover only 71% of quarters at its first assessable date.

## Market era  — *regime (universe.REGIMES)*

One of six named stretches between 2011 and 2025 with distinct conditions — the oil crash, the retail decline, the pandemic, the rate shock, and two others.

**Why it matters:** A tool that only spots trouble when the whole market is falling has detected the market, not the company. Requiring hits across several eras — including 2017-19, when there was no broad downturn to ride — is the check against that. It is one of the few criteria the tool did pass: it found companies in all six.

**Example:** 2017-19-idiosyncratic is described in the code as the most informative window in the set precisely because there was no market-wide tide to explain a hit.

## Companies that did not go wrong  — *control group (is_positive=False cases)*

The comparison set: companies that were run through exactly the same process and whose later filings show no confirmed bad turn.

**Why it matters:** Without them there is no way to tell a warning system from a system that warns about everything. Any alert on a control company is by definition a false alarm. They are generated automatically across the whole eligible universe, not hand-picked, so nobody chose which companies would be easy.

**Example:** The sealed test used 209 control companies across 7,657 company-quarters.

## Practice set and sealed test set  — *tuning split / holdout split (split.json, sha256-pinned)*

The companies are divided in two: one half to develop and tune the method on, the other half sealed and scored exactly once at the end.

**Why it matters:** Any method can be adjusted until it looks good on the data used to build it. The sealed half is the only honest measurement, and the division is fixed by a fingerprint committed to version control — if the file changes, the test is void and the code refuses to proceed.

**Example:** Weights and cut-offs were fitted on 18,480 practice company-quarters. The sealed half was then scored once, on 30 August 2026, and the result stands.

## The pass mark, written down first  — *pre-registered decision rule (data/prereg.json)*

The exact standard the method had to meet was written to a file and committed before the test was run, and the code refuses to overwrite it.

**Why it matters:** Deciding what counts as success after seeing the results is how almost every method ends up looking better than it is. Fixing the bar in advance is what makes a failure meaningful — and this method failed a bar it set for itself.

**Example:** The committed rule: catch at least 60% of the bad turns, at most 4% false alarms per company-quarter, at least 6 months of median warning, hits in at least 4 eras, and beat a trivial comparison method. Fail any one and the answer is KILL.

## False alarm rate  — *false positive rate per quarter (0.0383) and per filer (0.512)*

How often the tool raises an alarm on a company that turned out fine — counted both per company-quarter checked and per company overall.

**Why it matters:** The two numbers tell very different stories and both are published. Per quarter it looks small and passed its 4% bar. Per company it is the number a reader actually feels: more than half of the perfectly healthy companies got flagged at least once.

**Example:** On the sealed test, roughly 293 alarms across 7,657 healthy company-quarters is 3.8% — within the 4% limit. But 107 of the 209 healthy companies, 51%, were flagged at some point.

## Share of bad turns actually caught  — *positive hit rate (0.287 vs min_positive_hit_rate 0.60)*

Of the companies that genuinely deteriorated and could fairly be assessed, the fraction the tool warned about in time.

**Why it matters:** This is the number that decides whether the tool is useful, and it is the one that failed. It caught roughly 47 of 164, about 29%, against a bar of 60% written down beforehand. In plain terms: it misses about seven of every ten problems it was built to find. Nothing else about the method rescues that.

**Example:** 164 assessable companies that went on to deteriorate; 47 of them flagged early enough to count. 47/164 = 0.287.

## How early the warning came  — *lead time (Outcome.lead_months, MAX_CREDITABLE_LEAD_MONTHS = 24)*

The number of months between the tool raising an alarm and the day the bad news actually reached the public in a filing.

**Why it matters:** A warning that arrives after the news is a summary, not a warning. Measured against the filing date rather than the period covered, because a quarter ending 31 March is not public knowledge until it is filed in May. Anything more than 24 months early is not credited, because a fire years beforehand is unrelated rather than prescient. Where the tool did fire correctly, the median warning was 9 months.

**Example:** Alarm raised 15 August 2021; the quarter that confirmed the deterioration was filed 10 May 2022. Lead time: 9 months.

## Warning may have started before we could look  — *censored (Outcome.censored)*

When a company sets off the alarm on the very first date it was possible to assess it, the true first warning is somewhere off the front of the record and cannot be dated.

**Why it matters:** A company that has been flagged since the day the data began is not being detected, it is just permanently flagged — and counting that as a brilliantly early catch is exactly how earlier versions of this tool flattered themselves. These cases are reported and then set aside from both the hit rate and the timing, rather than being counted either way.

**Example:** 14 of the 178 companies in the sealed test that deteriorated fired on their first-ever assessable date, so they were excluded from both scores and reported separately.

## The obvious-check comparison  — *must_beat_baseline: ttm_ocf_negative_and_net_debt_positive*

A deliberately crude rule — the company is burning cash and owes more than it holds — that this whole method had to beat to justify existing.

**Why it matters:** Anyone can apply the crude rule in ten seconds without any of this machinery. On the sealed test the crude rule raised false alarms on 0.5% of healthy company-quarters and this method raised them on 3.8%, so it lost. That is the second of the two criteria it failed.

**Example:** Crude rule false alarm rate 0.0051; this method 0.0383 — roughly seven times as many false alarms.

## The machine-readable version of the filings  — *XBRL / companyfacts*

Every figure comes from the tagged data companies are required to file with the SEC, which is the same numbers as the published report in a form a computer can read exactly.

**Why it matters:** Nothing here is estimated, scraped from prose, or supplied by a model. Every number carries the filing it came from, so any figure the tool prints can be traced back to the document a company actually filed.

**Example:** A flag on days-to-get-paid links to the specific 10-Q accession number containing the receivables figure it used.
